# Palmpay sales analysis: speech

About 8 minutes of talking at a steady pace of 140 words a minute, plus pauses to point at charts and a short question break. That fills a 10-minute slot. Each section names its slide and the assessment question it answers. Every figure was checked against the Power BI model.

## Slide 1: Title (about 15 seconds)

Thank you for having me. I'm Brandy Sango. I'm going to take you through my answers to the Palmpay assessment: five years of sales in two countries, what happened, and what I would do about it.

## Slide 2: Data and method (about 40 seconds)

The data is one sales sheet covering January 2007 to December 2011, from the United States and Germany.

The first thing I checked was currency. The sales are not recorded in one currency. German sales are recorded in euros, and the euro totals come out lower than the dollar totals, while the US figures are the same in both. So every number I show is in US dollars, which is the only way to compare the two countries fairly.

Where a question tells me to exclude Silicon Valley Bikes, I did, and I'll say so each time. Everywhere else it's included.

## Slide 3: Overview (about 50 seconds)

Over the five years Palmpay sold about 169,000 items and earned $285 million in revenue. Gross profit was $149 million, a margin of 52.3 percent.

That's a healthy business on the surface. The rest of this talk is about what sits underneath those totals.

This is the Executive Overview page of the report I built in Power BI. The four cards at the top show the headline numbers, and the first two carry a twelve-month trend line. The chart below plots every city's revenue against its margin, and the Play button steps through the years so you can watch the cities move. The report has twelve pages in all, and I'll point to the ones that answer each question.

## Slide 4: The two countries (about 95 seconds): Section A, questions 1 and 2

The two countries moved in opposite directions.

Germany grew every year, from $31.0 million in 2007 to $35.1 million in 2011.

The United States started at $29.8 million, slipped to $28.0 million in 2008, then fell to $20.8 million in 2009. That is a drop of about 26 percent in a single year. It has stayed near $21.5 million ever since, about $8 million a year below where it began. Units tell the same story: US units fell from 19,383 to 13,115, about a third fewer.

What can I say about the reasons? Two things. The timing: the fall begins in 2008 and 2009, which is when the financial crisis hit. And discounting: the US cut its average discount from $200 to $171 and volume did not come back, while Germany raised its discount from $123 to $146 and kept growing.

On the page itself, the revenue lines show the gap opening between the two countries, with a dashed trend line for each. There is also a switcher above the chart, so I can flip the same view between revenue, units sold and gross margin without leaving the page.

What the data cannot tell me is why. The crisis is the likely explanation, not a proven one, and I don't want to claim more than the numbers support.

## Slide 5: The 2008 crisis (about 60 seconds): Section A, question 3

For this question I removed Silicon Valley Bikes and compared each month with the same month a year earlier.

Until August 2008, sales were within about six percent of the year before. Then, in September 2008, the month Lehman Brothers collapsed, sales were 11.6 percent lower. The fall deepened to 20 percent in November and reached 24 percent in January 2009. The first month back above the year before was September 2009.

Almost all of that was the United States, down as much as 46 percent in January 2009. Germany's worst month in those two years was minus 9.8 percent.

This page puts the two countries side by side on the same scale, so the difference is easy to see. Put one chart on top of the other and the German line looks almost flat. That is the point: the shock was real, but it landed on one market.

## Slide 6: Top seller (about 15 seconds): Section B, question 2

Touring bikes provided the most revenue in every year, between $20.6 million and $24.0 million. That is 37 to 40 percent of all revenue. Road bikes and off-road bikes follow closely behind.

## Slide 7: The flop (about 30 seconds): Section B, question 1

The flop is the Fixed Gear Bike Plus. It sold the fewest units in every year: 21, 17, 10, 10 and 14. That's 72 units in five years. The next slowest, the City Bike Max, sold between 59 and 96 a year.

I counted only products that sold in that year, so the E-Bike, which first appears in 2010, isn't counted as a zero before it launched.

## Slide 8: Off-road bikes by city (about 30 seconds): Section B, question 3

In 2011, off-road bikes were 7,266 of the 19,393 bicycles sold. That is 37.5 percent. I counted every category except accessories as bicycles.

Across cities the average share is 36.9 percent. Three cities stand clearly above it: München at 48.7 percent, Denver at 48.0 and Irvine at 41.1. These are the places where off-road bikes matter most, so they're where off-road stock and promotion should go first.

## Slide 9: Seasonality (about 60 seconds): Section B, question 4

The expectation is that bikes and accessories sell in spring and summer. The data supports it. 54 percent of all revenue arrives in April, May and June, and only 9 percent from November to February. Every product but one peaks in June.

The exception is the First Aid Kit. Only about half its revenue falls between March and August, and it peaks in July. It sells close to evenly through the year. That is the product lacking seasonality, and it is a natural one to carry the quiet months.

In the report you can pick any product on the left and see its own monthly line against the average. Press Play and it steps through the years. The shape barely changes from year to year, which tells me the pattern is stable and not a one-off.

## Slide 10: Recommendations (about 65 seconds): Section B, question 5

I have five recommendations.

One: win back US customers. Find out which customers stopped buying after 2008 and target them with retention and win-back offers. That is the largest gap in the business.

Two: rethink US discounts. Test bigger discounts on touring and road bikes, and track what they do to margin before rolling anything out.

Three: keep investing in touring, road and off-road bikes. They make up almost all revenue, so stock and marketing should follow them.

Four: review the Fixed Gear Bike Plus. It sold 72 units in five years. Either relaunch it with a clear plan or drop it.

Five: plan around the season. Build stock before April, and run promotions from November to February, where the First Aid Kit and accessories can carry sales.

I put them in this order because the first is the biggest gap and the last is the easiest to start. None of them needs new data to begin.

## Slide 11: Conclusion (about 50 seconds)

Palmpay's business is sound but uneven. Germany and the three main bike lines carry it, and the United States shows how exposed the company was to the 2008 crisis. The priorities are to win back US customers, protect the touring, road and off-road lines, and plan stock around the April to June peak.

Two limits are worth saying out loud. This is five years of data from two countries, and it ends in 2011, so I would want more recent figures before committing budget. And the data shows what happened, not why customers behaved as they did, so the first step of any win-back plan is to ask them.

Thank you. I'm happy to take questions.

## Likely questions

**Why did you exclude Silicon Valley Bikes?** The assessment asked for it in question A3, so I applied it only there. Every other figure includes all customers.

**Why US dollars?** Sales are recorded in more than one currency. German sales are recorded in euros and come out lower than the dollar totals, and US sales are the same in both. The dollar columns make the two countries comparable.

**How did you define the flop?** The product with the fewest units sold in each year, counting only products that sold in that year.

**Does the discount trend prove anything?** No. It shows what happened alongside the sales, not what caused it. The US cut its discount while volume stayed low, and Germany raised its discount while it grew. I would test discounts before relying on either pattern.

**Is the crisis the cause of the US fall?** The timing fits, and the fall is far larger in the US than in Germany. The data cannot prove the cause.

**What would you do first?** Win back US customers. US revenue is about $8 million a year below 2007, and no other gap in the business is as large.

**Why is the Fixed Gear Bike Plus worth discussing if it is so small?** It is the clearest product decision in the data. A product that sells 72 units in five years either needs a plan or should be dropped.
